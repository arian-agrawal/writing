# Notes from Jason Rathje on Selling to the Government

We hosted Jason Rathje at SPC NYC for a lunch and roundtable with founders building in defense, space, and dual-use. Jason has seen government buying from nearly every side. He started AFWERX's experimental SBIR program at the Air Force, founded the Office of Strategic Capital at the Pentagon, and now runs public sector at webAI, which builds small models that run on-device.

Most founders treat "the government" as one customer. The most useful thing Jason did was break it apart.

## Know which money you're asking for

There are three buckets, and they behave nothing alike.

**R&D money** (basic research through applied development) is for technology that isn't ready yet, roughly TRL 5 and below. The government funds it. It doesn't buy it.

**Procurement money** is what most founders picture when they think of a big contract. It needs a line in the congressional budget, and new technology takes at least two years to show up in the President's budget. If your plan depends on procurement dollars next year, you don't have a plan.

**O&M money** (operations and maintenance) is the most flexible and the most underrated. It's discretionary to the buyer. You can sell a commercial product directly, no program of record required.

webAI deliberately runs about half its business selling directly to the government and half as a supplier to the primes. The goal is to get the government to validate the technology before the big wave of demand hits.

## SBIRs are a numbers game

SBIRs are the front door, not the destination. Phase 1 is capped around $250K and Phase 2 around $1.7M, and you can stack several Phase 2s. STRATFI, which is now law, goes further: up to $15M in SBIR funding plus up to $15M in matched government funding, as long as you raise private capital to match it one to one.

A few mechanics most founders miss:

- **The government evaluates the proposal, not the company.** Apply to as many as you credibly can. A strong company hits about 20% of the ones it targets.
- **STTRs get accepted more often than SBIRs.** They require an academic partner who takes 30 to 40% of the money, but the odds are better.
- **Open topics beat traditional topics.** At AFWERX, open topics accepted 25 to 30% of proposals versus about 10% for traditional ones.
- **The statute is about economic growth, not science.** The program exists to make small companies big. Pitch it that way.

## The user is not the buyer

The operator who loves your product almost never controls the budget. A separate organization does the buying, and you need advocates inside it willing to fight for you through a full budget cycle.

Hardware is much easier to sell than software. The government doesn't like software licenses and prefers to buy services. One workaround that works: sell the rugged laptop with your software already on it.

On outreach, Jason was blunt. Government officials legally can't answer an unsolicited DM without answering everyone, so cold LinkedIn messages go nowhere. Conferences work. So do SBIR offices, which are generally responsive.

And hire people who have spent 15 to 20 years in contracting or program management. Advisors with only operational backgrounds often don't know how acquisition actually works.

## Prescribe or discover?

The popular defense narrative says the winners walked in and told the military what it needed. Jason thinks that's partly revisionist. He points out that Anduril's first SBIR was a small Phase 1 he awarded, and that the real ideation happened after the company started working with government users, not before.

The open topic model was built for exactly this. Companies pitch freely, get matched with real users, and apply for Phase 2 jointly with that user. The path from a $50K Phase 1 to a $15 to $30M Phase 3 takes about four to five years. You can show them the car instead of a faster horse, but you still have to sit down with the people who buy cars.

## The gaps he wishes he'd closed

Two things Jason didn't finish at OSC are still open problems:

- **Working capital for manufacturers in the $10 to $20M range.** SBA loans top out around $5 to $10M and require personal guarantees, which venture investors hate. Nothing fills that gap for venture-backed manufacturers.
- **Convertible debt for strategic bets.** His example: if DOE's $465M loan to Tesla had carried a small convertible stake, selling it at IPO would have covered the government's Solyndra losses. The hard parts are the optics of the government holding equity and finding people who can run it cleanly. There's precedent, though: the government took equity in banks and airlines in 2008.

## Talk to Congress before you need to

Founders usually wait until Series B or C to think about policy. Jason argues that's too late.

Meet your local representatives and tell them what you're building and why it matters for national security. Get to know the staffers, who are often in their mid-twenties and are the real connective tissue. For defense, the four committees that matter are House and Senate Armed Services and House and Senate Appropriations.

AI regulation is coming, and it may arrive at the state and city level before anything federal. The founders who show up early as good actors will help write it.

There's a structural problem underneath all of this: "startup" has no legal definition. Under SBA programs a venture-backed company is treated the same as the local ice cream shop. Until that changes, founders have to make the case for themselves. Venture firms underinvest in policy work, and it's one of the highest-leverage things they could do for their companies.

## What actually predicts success

Jason named two traits: **endurance and domain expertise.** Government sales cycles are long and the feedback is ambiguous, so you need to be comfortable waiting. And deep technical expertise in your field is what earns credibility with government evaluators.

Beyond that: be transparent, approachable, humble, and credible. Know whether you're a defense company selling weapons systems or a dual-use company, because the playbooks differ. And stay aggressive in your category. Microsoft became the Navy's dominant operating system provider in the 1990s despite a famously public failure at sea.

The founders who win in government aren't always the ones with the best demo. They're the ones who understand which money they're asking for, who controls it, and how long they're willing to wait.
