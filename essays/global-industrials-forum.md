# Notes from the Global Industrials Forum

I went to the Global Industrials Forum expecting a day talking about relatively consensus themes around compute, data centers and the bottleneck around energy. But the group that the Nasdaq team assembled allowed us to go deeper on the specific challenges that industrials are seeing.

We know that power is the constraint, but it was clear how understated that concern actually is. US data centers used about 4.4% of the country's electricity in 2023. By 2030 the projections put them at 12%. The hyperscalers have approximated $725B of capex for 2026 alone with a full buildout running past $3 trillion by the end of the decade. You cannot pour that kind of money into compute without first answering a much more boring question: where does the electricity actually come from, and what carries it.

The boring questions are now top of mind across the entire stack from builders, to energy providers, to tech and politicians, all of whom were onsite for this conference.

We are trying to build the most advanced computing infrastructure in history on top of a grid held together by parts most people have never thought about, and a lot of those parts we don't make here anymore.

The AI story everyone tells is a software story with a chip or GPU emphasis. The story the people in that room are living is an industrial story. The industry is quietly shifting from electrical to optical connectivity because it's the only way to keep up with the speed and power demands. And underneath all of it, a labor problem nobody likes to talk about: an industry building flat out with a workforce that has never built at this scale.

So the demand is obvious. The question is where does the money go now?

A lot of it is going to defense, and for good reason. Defense tech investment doubled year over year. The government is an active buyer and deploying real money. The culture has genuinely changed too; acquisition officers are now the ones reaching out to startups instead of the other way around.

Consensus capital piles into the legible opportunity, and right now the legible opportunity is a defense startup with a slick demo and a couple of letters of intent. When everyone can see the same bet, the returns get competed away before the hard part is even built.

One interesting take away was the bet on Materials. It's the least glamorous and most foundational piece of the entire reindustrialization push, and it's still genuinely hard to invest in. There's no clean playbook, no SaaS multiple to point at, no quick exit. But it's where government money is starting to move, and it's the foundation for the next ten years of everything else.

Here's why it matters more than it sounds. If energy is the base layer of national sovereignty, and it is, then we have a problem. We depend on China for solar panels and for rare earth processing. You can stack compute, manufacturing, defense, and space on top, but you cannot build a sovereign technology stack on a base layer you don't control. Materials are that base layer. They're upstream of the grid, upstream of the data center, upstream of the weapons system. And they're the part almost nobody is funding.

States are moving faster than the federal government in some cases. We discussed in our panel how Michigan put up a billion dollars and is handing the National Guard's 30,000 square feet over for drone testing. Financing is getting more creative too, blending state, private, and federal capital, and people are openly arguing that infrastructure companies should go public earlier, post Series B, because venture capital is too expensive for buildouts this heavy and public markets are valuing defense industrials well right now.

When it comes to data centers, the objectives are also getting solved, slowly. The water problem is mostly a perception problem at this point; modern facilities run closed-loop and skip the cooling towers entirely. New York is weighing a twelve-month moratorium. The companies that win will be the ones doing town halls and exporting power back to the grid during outages, not the ones treating a town as a site.

For a long time the most ambitious people I knew were building software because that's where the leverage was. The leverage is moving. The hard, physical, deeply unglamorous problems are back at the center of the most important industry of the decade, and the foundation of all of it.

But the group the Nasdaq team assembled let us go several layers deeper into the specific, operational challenges industrials are seeing in real time: what breaks first, what takes the longest to fix, and what "capacity" actually means when you try to build at hyperscale.

You cannot pour that kind of money into compute without first answering a much more boring question: where does the electricity actually come from, and what carries it—through interconnect queues, transformer lead times, substation buildouts, and transmission constraints that don't care about your product roadmap.

It's not just "more megawatts." It's interconnection studies, land and permitting, gas turbine availability, renewable intermittency and storage, and the hard reality that upgrading a grid is measured in years, not quarters. The friction is everywhere: equipment backlogs, right-of-way constraints, and a regulatory process designed for a world that wasn't trying to electrify everything at once.
