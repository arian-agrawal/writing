# What is GTM in Deeptech?

When I was working on my last company, talking to customers was the easy part, and honestly the fun part. I could message someone, host them in our office / showroom, build something over a weekend and get real feedback almost instantly to shape our product and decisions.

Since joining SPC I have had the privilege of working with founders across various domains in deeptech. I realized that I had to unlearn and create new definitions of my own intuition before I could convey the need to speak to customers. The deep tech founder experience is quite literally close to the inverse of mine. They often don't have a product to put in front of anyone and their buyer might be a utility or a regulator whose sales cycle runs in years. Science has to work. When I tell one of them to think about go-to-market, the honest reaction is that it feels premature, maybe even a little dishonest.

That reaction is correct, as long as you're using the consumer definition of GTM, and most technical founders are without realizing it. In software and consumer, go-to-market means selling. You have something, the open question is execution, and traction is the answer. Deep tech founders inherit that definition, look at their own situation, and freeze. By that standard they have nothing to sell for years.

So here's the reframe I had to find for myself, and it's the most useful thing I know about this stage.

For deep tech, go-to-market isn't a proxy for revenue. It's a proxy for fundability. The job of GTM right now isn't to get early traction, or small figure contracts to demonstrate demand. The job of GTM is to make you legible to the person who decides whether you survive to the next round.

## What kills a deep tech company?

The thing that kills early deep tech companies is rarely the physics. It's running out of money before you can prove the one thing that unlocks the next tranche to buy you time to keep building. For a stretch you're a science project, which means your ability to raise isn't a nice-to-have on top of the technology. It's part of the technology's survival path.

Ultimately, fundability is existential. At some point you have to convince a larger fund to write a check north of fifty million dollars, and if you can't, you die. A billion-dollar fund writing that check is underwriting a fifteen-billion-dollar outcome. At that scale they're not pricing your early technical risk. They're pricing the story and whether you're the team positioned to own it. Everything you do in the first couple of years is really about earning the right to have that conversation. At the same time, funding will be impossible the first few rounds. Often times, deep tech companies only get 1 term sheet for their first 1-4 rounds of funding.

## So what does GTM actually mean? Find the one buyer that actually cares enough to talk to you today.

The instinct most technical founders have is to say the whole world needs this. While sure, that's maybe true, it's also completely useless. The entire exercise at this stage is to make the target narrower and narrower until you're building something close to bespoke for a single customer.

How do you do this? Start with the superset of everyone who could ever use the technology, then leave it behind. Burn down to the domains that actually feel the pain. Find the one segment where this is urgent rather than interesting. Then go have real conversations with those potential buyers. Step one is honestly worthless. The value is all in the narrowing, and in getting real data points from derived customer insights to support that.

## What counts before you have contracts

You won't have signed revenue in three to six months, maybe longer. The good news, and the reality is that seed and Series A deep tech investors don't expect it. What they expect is a sharp answer to who the customer is and why they care. There's a ladder you can climb without a single contract, and each rung makes you more fundable than the last.

The bottom rung is a real, validated pain point, with evidence rather than a thesis you assert. Above that is naming the hair-on-fire segment that feels it most. Above that is engaged discovery with a specific buyer who'll vouch for the pain and the ROI in their own words. Near the top are concrete letters of intent, the kind with real volumes and price thresholds, not the performative kind a founder collects just to say they have them. Hitting the upper rungs, even with zero firm contracts, is often enough to raise.

You can go further and pull future demand into the present. Carbon capture buyers pre-purchase credits. Sustainable aviation fuel companies sign offtake deals that say hit this cost and we buy this volume. Demand committed today funds the build. And when you do get to design partners, make them carry the capital cost of early deployments. Don't burn venture equity subsidizing hardware you put in the field. Software founders never have to, and you've got more leverage here than you think.

## The part founders hate

All of this eventually comes back to a single uncomfortable fact. Most deep tech investors are, honestly, the least technical people in the room. You can't win them with a thesis defense or a black box. So your job, alongside building, is to make the work legible: speak in plain language, validate with buyers instead of academic peers, and know the landscape well enough to say why yours matters among the ten best-sounding companies they've already seen.

Which gets to the bottom line. Take two founders with comparable technology. The one who can narrow the buyer, gather the evidence, and make the bet legible is the one who raises the round and wins the market, even when the underlying tech isn't the very best in the field. When investors can't judge the technology, they back the best salesperson.

The customer conversations I found so easy in consumer are still the job here. They just don't come to you, and they don't pay off for a while. But none of the work above requires a finished product.

## Pitch top-down, and own the risk instead of hiding it

When you do pitch, resist the urge to start from the bottom with your architecture. Generalist investors, who control most of the capital, have no shared anchor points in your domain. There's no standard benchmark, no familiar comp set. If you open with technical detail you're forcing them to build context from scratch, and most won't bother.

Start with the macro trend and your belief about where the world is going, then show how your technology uniquely captures it. The details are supporting evidence, not the headline. Not "we build ten to twenty kilowatt satellite buses," but "if we get the most power to orbit, the way data centers have the most power on earth, we win."

The other trap is making your risks feel small. It backfires. A non-technical investor reads minimized risk as something you're hiding, and it triggers skepticism. Name the risks plainly, then make it feel inevitable that when they're resolved, the company is enormous. Owning the risk and pairing it with outsized upside is what actually builds conviction.
